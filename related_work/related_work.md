# Related work

## Classical accounts of growth (area 1)

Network-growth theory supplies the deliberately simple mechanisms that AGN uses as candidate experts. The Barabási--Albert model assigns new edges with probability increasing in accumulated degree, producing a cumulative-advantage account of scale-free structure \cite{barabasi1999emergence}. General attachment-kernel work shows that the functional relation between degree and attractiveness need not be fixed or linear \cite{krapivsky2000connectivity}. Fitness models instead allow nodes to differ in inherent attractiveness; the Bianconi--Barabási formulation distinguishes first-mover, fit-get-rich, and winner-take-all regimes \cite{bianconi2001bose}. Aging models make a node's attractiveness decline with its age \cite{dorogovtsev2000aging}. For citations, Wang, Song, and Barabási combine mechanisms to describe a paper's subsequent citation trajectory \cite{wang2013impact}.

Closure and copying supply different local explanations. Holme and Kim introduce a growth process with tunable clustering through triad formation \cite{holme2002tunable}. Copying models choose a prototype and reproduce part of its links; Krapivsky and Redner give a copying mechanism that can generate sparse scale-free networks \cite{krapivsky2005copying}. These models are generative explanations of an aggregate graph. AGN differs in conditioning on an *observed new node's features* and an explicit historical candidate set, ranking its whole outgoing reference set rather than simulating an unconditional network. Its memory expert is only analogous to copying: it attends over several recent, feature-similar births and aggregates the references they selected, instead of copying a randomly selected prototype with a fixed probability. Its popularity, recent-popularity, age, and similarity scores are not claimed to be faithful estimators of the corresponding physical mechanisms.

## Estimating and comparing mechanisms (areas 2--3)

The closest statistical literature treats formation as a sequence of choices. Overgoor, Benson, and Ugander formulate every new edge as a conditional multinomial-logit choice from an available set. Their framework makes preferential attachment, triadic closure, and fitness special cases, permits feature significance testing, and discusses mixture estimation \cite{overgoor2019choosing}. Importantly, their citation example conditions on an edge initiator and evaluates held-out choices. Gupta and Porter develop repeated-choice/mixed-logit models for formation and argue that this relaxes important limitations of multinomial logit \cite{gupta2022mixed}. This is substantially closer to AGN than generic temporal link prediction: it provides a likelihood-based, interpretable way to estimate how target attributes affect a new node's choices.

AGN shares the conditional-choice framing but is not presently a discrete-choice model. It uses a listwise discriminative ranking loss, a deliberately restricted candidate generator, feature-conditioned nonlinear gates, and a learned attention memory. Thus its gate weights cannot be read as logit coefficients, effect sizes, posterior mechanism probabilities, or causal contributions. A direct discrete-choice comparison must be added before mechanism claims are credible.

Mechanism identifiability is a central warning rather than a minor caveat. Golosovsky shows that commonly used degree-growth evidence does not by itself discriminate preferential attachment from fitness and develops a memory-based recursive-search model \cite{golosovsky2018mechanisms}. Touwen et al. train a classifier on simulations from nine combinations of preferential attachment, fitness, and aging, using interpretable dynamic features for model selection; their citation application is explicitly cautious about feature-dependent selections \cite{touwen2024learning}. AGN differs by predicting individual births rather than selecting one network-level generative class. Conversely, those works show why a successful AGN ranking result alone would not demonstrate a universal or uniquely identified growth law. Cross-domain conclusions need simulation recovery tests, alternative feature definitions, uncertainty intervals, and comparisons to models with interactions such as closure.

## Temporal neural link prediction and memory (areas 4 and 7)

Temporal GNNs learn event-conditioned representations primarily to predict future interactions. JODIE uses coupled recurrent updates and projects an entity's embedding forward in time \cite{kumar2019jodie}. DyRep uses a two-timescale point process and temporally attentive representations for dynamic link and event-time prediction \cite{trivedi2019dyrep}. TGAT is explicitly inductive: temporal attention and functional time encodings infer representations for both observed and new nodes \cite{xu2020tgat}. TGN is a modular framework with per-node memory, messages, temporal embedding, and graph operators; its reported evaluation includes transductive and inductive tasks \cite{rossi2020tgn}. More recent transformer variants include DyFormer, which uses spatial--temporal encoding and self-supervised objectives \cite{cong2022dyformer}, and DyGFormer, which uses historical first-hop sequences, neighbour co-occurrence encoding, and sequence patching \cite{yu2023dygformer}.

These methods overlap materially with AGN: all can use temporal history and node features to score links, and TGN-style memory makes the phrase ``attention over history'' insufficiently novel by itself. AGN differs architecturally and scientifically in placing a bounded, birth-time memory alongside named classical score experts, with a gate indexed by the arriving paper. Typical temporal-GNN link-prediction papers neither require one newly arriving node to choose its complete set of references from a fixed historical candidate set nor expose a mixture over popularity, fitness, recency, text similarity, and copying as their primary result. That distinction is useful only if it survives a head-to-head evaluation against inductive TGAT/TGN/DyGFormer implementations under exactly the same candidate set and temporal cutoff.

Benchmark work also changes the burden of evaluation. TGB supplies diverse temporal datasets and realistic evaluation protocols \cite{huang2023tgb}; TGB-Seq reports that standard temporal models can fail when repeated links are suppressed and sequential structure matters \cite{yi2025tgbseq}. AGN's proposed per-paper Recall@K is appropriate for multi-reference births, but it should report candidate recall ceilings, test on unseen-node conditions, and separate repeated-edge advantages from genuine generalization.

## Neural graph generation (area 5)

Generative graph models address a neighbouring but different target. GraphRNN autoregressively generates node and edge sequences conditioned on the graph generated so far \cite{you2018graphrnn}. TIGGER combines temporal point processes with autoregressive modelling and offers transductive and inductive variants for temporal interaction graphs \cite{gupta2022tigger}. Both demonstrate that neural models can represent growth histories and, in TIGGER, scale across time and network size. Neither is a content-conditioned reference recommender with a predeclared set of mechanism experts. Conversely, AGN is not a full graph generator: it conditions on actual node arrivals, does not model arrival times or reference counts, and ranks a restricted candidate set. Claims of generative fidelity would therefore require new likelihood and graph-statistic evaluations.

## Citation recommendation and science forecasting (areas 6 and 8)

Citation recommendation directly shares AGN's prediction object. Bhagavatula et al. embed a draft, retrieve nearest-neighbour candidate papers, and discriminatively rerank observed versus unobserved citations using content; their approach can embed newly seen documents without author metadata \cite{bhagavatula2018citation}. AGN adds temporal eligibility, hand-specified growth features, and a prototype-reference memory, but it must compare against a strong content retriever/reranker before attributing gains to growth mechanisms. In particular, a flexible text model could subsume much of the similarity and memory signal.

Science-of-science forecasting commonly predicts a different edge. Science4Cast predicts future co-occurrences between concepts in an evolving AI semantic network and finds carefully engineered network features competitive with end-to-end methods \cite{krenn2023science4cast}. Sourati and Evans forecast discoveries using models that incorporate the distribution of human expertise, rather than research content alone \cite{sourati2023humanaware}. These works motivate cross-domain temporal testing, but their nodes and targets are concepts or discoveries, not an arriving paper's outgoing citations. AGN should not claim to forecast scientific impact, discovery, or research directions merely from reference-prediction performance.

## Gated experts and interpretation (area 9)

Mixture-of-experts layers route inputs among specialized models. Chen et al. analyse a sparsely activated MoE controlled by a router and show, in their setting, why a mixture can solve some problems that a single expert cannot \cite{chen2022moe}. This establishes neither that AGN's expert labels are identifiable nor that a gate is an explanation. AGN's stronger distinction is the *semantic predefinition* of four classical growth scores plus a copying-like birth memory, all mixed per new node. The resulting weights are useful descriptive diagnostics and motivate falsifiable ablations (global mixture and no-memory), but they should be labelled as model-internal readouts. Establishing a mechanism interpretation requires calibration against known synthetic mixtures, stability across seeds and candidate construction, and agreement with a discrete-choice estimator.
